Under Pressure Online

Three ways social media can weigh on our minds: measuring ourselves against others, scrolling through bad news, and being afraid to say what we think. Each section has the key ideas, real cases, practical tips, and articles to keep reading.

Topic 1

The Comparison Trap

Psychologists have known since Leon Festinger’s 1954 theory that people judge themselves by comparing themselves to others. Social media makes this constant: we see a “highlight reel” of other people’s lives, so it is easy to feel behind. The most harmful kind is upward comparison, looking at people who seem better off than we are.

r = .54Link between upward comparison tendency and body dissatisfaction in a 2026 meta-analysis (preprint)
6–9%Share of self-esteem and depression differences explained by exposure to upward comparison in a 2025 young-adult study

Important nuance: the effects are real but modest, and results are mixed. A small McMaster University student survey even found that some participants reported positive outcomes after social media use.

Real case: Instagram’s own research

The Facebook Files (2021)

The Wall Street Journal reported on leaked internal studies showing Facebook’s researchers knew that comparison on Instagram was a problem. One slide said 32% of teen girls who felt bad about their bodies said Instagram made them feel worse. In the leaked research on appearance comparison, 48% of teen girls said they often compare their appearance to others, against 33% of all users. Instagram’s leaders replied that the research is mixed and that the company’s own findings mirror outside studies.

Recommendations

  1. Remember it’s a highlight reel. People post their best moments. What you see is curated, not typical.
  2. Practice gratitude. Researcher Mai-Ly Steers suggests gratitude is the antidote to comparison.
  3. Curate your feed. The studies point to content type and how often you compare as key factors, so mute accounts that make you feel worse.
  4. Watch your own state of mind. Instagram’s response noted that how people use social media and how they feel when they use it matters most. If you’re already feeling low, take a break.

Topic 2

Doomscrolling and Anxiety

Doomscrolling means compulsively consuming one bad news story or post after another. Our brains have a negativity bias, and news media often follow an “if it bleeds, it leads” approach, so the feed rarely runs out of things to worry about.

16.5%of 1,100 US adults showed “severely problematic” news consumption (Health Communication study)
74% vs 8%reported mental health problems in the severe group compared with everyone else

The researchers argued that it’s not how much news you consume but how: being absorbed in it, thinking about it constantly, and reading to calm anxiety (which backfires).

Real cases

800 students in the US and Iran (2024)

Researchers at Flinders University and colleagues found doomscrolling predicted higher existential anxiety in both countries. The effect was weak to moderate, and the authors note the sample was a convenience sample, so the findings are suggestive rather than final.

Melbourne’s COVID-19 lockdowns (2020)

A researcher studying Victorians under strict lockdowns found that people who consciously limited their news intake, without cutting it out completely, were better off and more relaxed at home.

Recommendations

  1. Notice how you feel. The authors of the 2024 study urge users to pay attention to their sensations, thoughts and feelings while scrolling.
  2. Limit, don’t eliminate. Partial avoidance worked for Victorians in lockdown. Stay informed without letting news take over your day.
  3. Ask why you’re checking. If you scroll to relieve anxiety, that is a warning sign. More news doesn’t make the feeling go away.
  4. Look for balance. Researchers encourage news that also reports positive outcomes of negative events.
  5. Be kind to yourself. Feeling stressed by bad news is a natural reaction. The goal is to notice when it becomes a problem.

Topic 3

Cancel Culture and Fear of Speaking

“Cancel culture” is a contested term. Some use it for holding people accountable; others for online pile-ons that punish people disproportionately. What many surveys agree on is a practical result: lots of people hold back their opinions. Keep in mind that the main surveys below come from polling groups and think tanks with their own viewpoints, so it helps to read more than one source.

55%said they’d held their tongue in the past year for fear of backlash (NYT/Siena poll, 2022)
62%said the political climate stops them from saying what they believe (Cato/YouGov, 2020)

The other side of the picture: a Politico poll cited alongside the Cato survey found that only 40% of voters said they had ever taken part in cancel culture, and just one in ten did so often. The loudest voices online may be a small minority.

Real case: Justine Sacco (2013)

One tweet, one flight

Justine Sacco, a PR executive with about 170 followers, posted a joke about AIDS and race before boarding a flight to South Africa. By the time she landed she had been denounced worldwide and soon lost her job. She said later she “really suffered.” She intended the joke as satire of privilege, but it was read as racist. Journalist Jon Ronson made her story central to his book So You’ve Been Publicly Shamed, arguing that online shaming can become disproportionate. Others, like workplace lawyer Josh Bornstein, point out that the company that fired her deserves scrutiny too.

Recommendations

  1. Pause before you post or pile on. Sacco’s case shows how quickly a message can travel without its context.
  2. Separate accountability from humiliation. Criticizing what someone said is different from trying to destroy them.
  3. Check the real numbers. If cancel culture is driven by a vocal minority, the fear may be bigger than the actual risk. Silence can make a minority look like a majority.
  4. Speak with context and care. Explain what you mean and be ready to clarify or apologize.
  5. Find safe spaces for honest talk. Friends, classmates or teachers who can disagree respectfully help you build confidence to speak up.
